About

I model biological matter at whatever scale the question needs, with the noise left in.

What first drew me to biology was an animation of kinesin walking along a microtubule. It was beautiful and it left something out. There was no water, no crowding, nothing knocking the motor off course. Real cytoplasm is dense, and everything in it is struck from every direction all the time. Molecular machines don't work despite that thermal noise. They work through it.

My research puts that noise back. I use quantum mechanics when the question is about electrons, such as how a drug forms a covalent bond with its target, and coarse-grained molecular dynamics when I need microseconds, such as how disordered regions of transcription factors help decide whether a cell becomes a heart cell or a neuron. I'm in the writing-up stage of my PhD at UCL and am building towards a biotech spin-out.

Cysteine reactivity and covalent inhibitor design

Covalent inhibition accounts for around 30% of marketed drugs, and cysteine is one of the most common targets. My PhD develops methods to determine how reactive a given cysteine is from its local protein environment, combining established and new pKa prediction methods with QM/MM simulations of known covalent inhibitors. The aim is to identify cysteines suited to covalent targeting, then understand how inhibitor design modulates that reactivity, for example in Michael addition warheads.

Transcription factor IDRs and condensates

Intrinsically disordered regions let transcription factors form condensates on chromatin. In my MPhil at the Cambridge Stem Cell Institute I built a model of NANOG–chromatin interaction dynamics, covering both homotypic and heterotypic interactions, to study how NANOG condensates drive long-range chromatin contacts during the acquisition and loss of naïve pluripotency. The model was informed by Hi-C, ChIP-seq and live single-molecule localisation microscopy. I continue this work on IDRs, condensates and post-translational modification kinetics.

Now · Green Room Campus, Ireland · Q4 2026

Privacy by noise: when deletion is no longer an option

Most consumer privacy tools assume data can be controlled: opt out, request erasure, delete the account. That stops working once a record has been copied, indexed, embedded and trained on. It matters most for biological data. You can change a password or a phone number, but you can't change your genome. When 23andMe went bankrupt in 2025, the genetic data of more than fifteen million people was sold as the main asset, two years after a breach had already exposed millions of users. Deleting your account afterwards does nothing about the copies.

For ten weeks I'm testing the opposite approach: lowering an adversary's confidence in what they hold by adding plausible decoys. For biographical data, the questions are empirical:

  • Which kinds of decoy work, and how much decoy volume is needed as the number of corroborating sources grows?
  • How does an adversary's confidence change as corroborating sources accumulate?
  • Can LLM-generated decoy records be told apart from real ones, and can they survive data-poisoning defences?

Genomic data is harder and more interesting. You can't fake a haplotype by flipping variants at random, because it fails phasing, linkage-disequilibrium decay and reference-panel checks. But the structure that makes decoys hard also makes the adversary's knowledge computable. Decoy generation becomes a constrained sampling problem on a manifold set by recombination, so decoys can go where they buy the most uncertainty per unit of budget. Because relatives expose each other, protection may have to be defined over a family or cohort, and I want to find that bound.

The real risk is cost. If the noise needed grows faster than linearly with the number of sources, this is a personal project rather than a scalable technology. Ten weeks is enough to find out either way.
